What is a castle?

A castle was a fortified private residence, built as much to project a lord's power over the surrounding land as to physically defend it. Most of the castles pictured today were built across Europe between the 11th and 15th centuries, and their design never stayed still for long: every generation adapted to whatever siege technology the last one had learned to defeat.

Know the structure

Castle Anatomy

A castle wasn't just a big building. Every structural feature had a specific defensive or practical job. Below are the key parts that made up a typical medieval castle, and why each one was necessary.

Photo: Richard Nevell, Lincoln Castle curtain wall, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Structure

Curtain Wall

The outer perimeter wall enclosing the castle grounds. It was built thick and tall so attackers couldn't easily breach it with battering rams or scale it quickly with ladders. It was the first physical barrier any attacking force had to get through.

Some curtain walls were built especially massive to withstand prolonged sieges. The walls at Caernarfon Castle in Wales reach over 3 metres thick in places, while Krak des Chevaliers in Syria used two complete rings of walls, so even if an attacker broke through the outer wall, a second one still stood in their way.

Photo: Mick Lobb, Tattershall Castle and moat, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

Approach

Moat

A deep ditch, often filled with water, surrounding the castle. It stopped siege towers and battering rams from reaching the walls directly, and forced attackers onto exposed drawbridges where defenders could pick them off.

Not all moats were flooded. Dry moats were common on hillside castles where digging deep enough to reach water wasn't possible, and the drop alone was enough to stop siege equipment. Bodiam Castle in England is one of the best-known examples of a wide, fully water-filled moat, largely because it also made the castle look impressive from a distance, not just defensible.

Photo: DeFacto, Dover Castle - Henry II's Great Tower, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Structure

Keep

The strongest, most heavily fortified building at the centre of the castle. If the outer defenses fell, the keep was the last line of defense, thick-walled and often supplied with its own food and water so defenders could hold out during a long siege.

The White Tower at the Tower of London is one of the most famous keeps still standing, built with walls up to 4.6 metres thick at the base. Keeps also often doubled as the residence of the lord who owned the castle, combining defense with everyday living space.

Photo: Hchc2009, Portcullis mechanism at Goodrich Castle, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Access

Gatehouse & Portcullis

The main entrance was the weakest point in any wall, so it was reinforced heavily. The portcullis, a heavy iron-reinforced wooden grille, could be dropped quickly to seal the entrance if attackers tried to rush the gate.

Larger castles often used twin-towered gatehouses with two portcullises, one at the front and one at the back, trapping any attackers who forced their way past the first in a narrow passage between them. Caerphilly Castle in Wales is a well-known example of this layered gatehouse design.

Photo: Andy Dingley, White Castle arrow slit, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Defense

Arrow Slits

Narrow openings in the walls, wide enough for a defender to fire arrows out but far too narrow for attackers to fire back in accurately. This let defenders attack safely from cover.

Later designs added a horizontal slit crossing the vertical one, forming a cross shape. This let archers angle their shots left and right as well as up and down, covering a much wider field of fire from the same fixed position.

Photo: Modulo, Lutsk Castle tower, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.5

Defense

Towers

Towers built at intervals along the curtain wall let defenders see and fire along the length of the wall itself, not just straight ahead. An attacker trying to climb the wall between two towers could be fired on from both sides at once. This is called flanking fire, and it's one of the main reasons medieval castles favoured round towers over flat, straight walls.

Conwy Castle in Wales is a strong example, built with eight large towers spaced evenly around its curtain wall so that no stretch of wall was ever out of a defender's line of sight.

Photo: Clem Rutter / Nev1, Rochester Castle, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Defense

Battlements (Crenellations)

The zig-zag pattern along the top of a wall, alternating raised sections (merlons) and gaps (crenels). Defenders could stand behind the raised sections for cover, then step into the gaps to fire down at attackers below.

Building crenellations without royal permission was actually restricted in medieval England. nobles had to be granted a "licence to crenellate," since fortifying your home too heavily without approval could be seen as preparing to rebel against the crown.

Photo: Christine Matthews, Bridge over Moat, Leeds Castle, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

Access

Drawbridge

A movable bridge over the moat that could be raised to seal off the entrance entirely. Combined with the portcullis behind it, this meant attackers faced two separate barriers just to reach the gate.

Some castles, like Leeds Castle in England, used multiple drawbridges across different sections of their moat, so even reaching the outer gate required crossing more than one controlled crossing point.

Attack shaped architecture

Why Castle Defenses Were Built

Every defensive feature on a castle existed because of a specific attack it needed to survive. Rather than looking at the parts on their own, this page looks at the actual siege tactics attackers used, and how castle defenses were designed directly in response to each one.

Photo: Clarinetlover, Medieval battering ram, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0

Direct Assault

Battering Rams & Siege Engines

A battering ram was simply a heavy log, swung again and again at a gate or wall until it gave way. Castles countered this threat with thick curtain walls and reinforced gatehouses, stone and timber built dense enough that repeated blows couldn't crack them before the attacking force ran out of time, supplies, or nerve.

Some walls were also built with a slight outward slope (a batter) at the base, making it harder for a ram crew to get a stable, direct swing at the stonework.

Pearson Scott Foresman, Scaling ladder, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Escalade

Ladder Scaling

Escalade, climbing straight over the wall with ladders, was often the fastest way to attack, but also the costliest. Moats kept ladder crews from ever reaching the base of the wall in the first place, while tall curtain walls meant a ladder had to be built long, awkward, and slow to raise under fire.

Battlements gave defenders standing cover to push ladders away from the wall the moment they were raised, often before a single attacker could climb them.

Medieval siege illustration, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain

Undermining

Mining & Sapping

Not every attack came from above ground. Sappers would dig tunnels beneath a wall, propping the tunnel up with wooden supports, then set the props alight. Once they burned through, the section of wall above collapsed. Square towers were especially vulnerable at their corners, which is a big part of why later castles shifted to round towers that were far harder to undermine cleanly.

Water-filled moats also helped here: digging a tunnel toward a wall was far harder when the ground around it was waterlogged or the moat itself would simply flood the tunnel.

Photo: Husond, Ptuj Castle courtyard well, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

Siege & Blockade

Starvation Sieges

Attackers didn't always need to breach a single wall. Surrounding a castle and waiting could be just as effective. Defenders countered this by building for self-sufficiency: a well inside the courtyard or keep meant a water supply that couldn't be cut off, and large storerooms let a garrison hold out for months.

Some sieges lasted well over a year for exactly this reason. The castle wasn't beaten by force, it simply had to be starved out slower than the besieging army could afford to wait.

Photo: Warwick Castle Trebuchet, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

Bombardment

Trebuchets & Siege Engines

Rather than sending soldiers to reach the walls, some attacks smashed them from a distance instead. Trebuchets used a massive counterweight to hurl boulders weighing hundreds of pounds at curtain walls and gatehouses from well beyond arrow range. Round towers were partly a response to exactly this threat. A curved surface deflects and scatters the force of a strike, where a flat wall takes it head-on.

At the 1304 siege of Stirling Castle, King Edward I reportedly had church roofs across Scotland stripped of their lead specifically to help build his trebuchets, assembling more than a dozen of them against a single castle.

Photo: Oast House Archive, Murder holes at Bodiam Castle, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

Breach

Rushing the Gate

If soldiers ever broke through the outer gate, the fight wasn't over. It just moved into a deliberately narrow, exposed passage. Murder holes, cut into the ceiling directly above this passage, let defenders fire arrows or drop stones and boiling substances onto attackers trapped below with nowhere to take cover.

That passage was often flanked by more arrow slits and a second portcullis further back, so soldiers rushing an open gate could find themselves boxed in on every side rather than through it.

Photo: SquireRamsalot, Caerphilly Castle hoarding exterior, via Wikimedia Commons, public domain (CC0)

Overhead Defense

Hoardings

Wooden hoardings were temporary fighting galleries built out from the top of a wall, giving defenders the same top-down firing position as a stone machicolation without it needing to be built permanently. Gaps in the hoarding's floor let defenders target attackers massed at the wall's base, including those trying to set the gate alight.

Being built from wood made hoardings vulnerable to fire themselves, which is part of why later castles increasingly replaced them with stone machicolations that couldn't be burned down partway through a siege.

Five centuries of adaptation

How Castle Design Changed Over Time

Castle design didn't stay still for 500 years. It was in a constant arms race with whatever siege technology existed at the time. Every major shift in castle architecture below happened because the previous generation of defenses had become beatable.

Photo: Chris Gunns, Beaudesert Motte and Bailey, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

c. 1000s

Motte-and-Bailey

The earliest castles weren't stone at all. A motte-and-bailey was a wooden tower on top of a raised earth mound (the motte), with an enclosed courtyard (the bailey) beside it, all surrounded by a ditch and wooden palisade. They were fast to build, often in just a few weeks, which mattered enormously to the Normans, who needed to secure conquered territory in England quickly after 1066.

The trade-off was durability. Wood rots, and it burns, and burning down the tower was often the simplest way to end a siege.

Photo: Peter Stack, Colchester Castle, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

c. 1000s–1100s

The Stone Keep

Once territory was secured, wood was rebuilt in stone. A stone keep couldn't be burned or hacked through, and its sheer scale (Colchester's keep is still the largest Norman keep in Europe) was as much a political statement as a defensive one, a visible sign that the new rulers were there to stay.

The downside was cost and time. A stone keep could take a decade or more to build, and its square corners were a structural weak point that later siege tactics, like mining, learned to exploit.

Photo: Jonathan Billinger, Beaumaris Castle moat, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0

c. 1200s–1300s

The Concentric Castle

By the 13th century, castle design had shifted away from a single dominant tower toward rings within rings, an outer wall lower than the inner one, so defenders on the inner wall could fire clean over the outer wall at the same time. Beaumaris Castle in Wales is considered the most complete example ever built.

This design removed the single-point weakness of the old keep entirely. There was no one tower to bring down that ended the siege. Attackers had to break through an entire ring, then do it again.

Fort Bourtange aerial view, via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0

c. 1400s onward

The Star Fort, and the End of the Castle

Gunpowder cannon changed everything. Tall, flat stone walls that had stopped battering rams for centuries could be smashed apart by cannon fire in hours. Fortifications responded by getting low, thick, and angled. Star-shaped bastion forts like Fort Bourtange replaced height with sloped earthwork walls that deflected cannonballs instead of absorbing them head-on.

This is really the end of the castle as a military structure. Star forts were built for cannon warfare, not knights and archers. The tall, romantic stone castle had simply been made obsolete by the same kind of arms race that built it in the first place.
